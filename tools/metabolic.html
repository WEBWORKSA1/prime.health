Metabolic health check

Five everyday measurements define metabolic syndrome. Three or more out of range roughly doubles the risk of heart disease and sharply raises diabetes risk.

How it works

The 2009 harmonized definition, agreed by the International Diabetes Federation, the American Heart Association, NHLBI and others, diagnoses metabolic syndrome when any three of these five are present:

  • Waist at or above the population cut-off (102 cm men / 88 cm women in US-style use; 90 / 80 cm for Asian populations)
  • Triglycerides 150 mg/dL (1.7 mmol/L) or more, or on treatment
  • HDL below 40 mg/dL (1.0 mmol/L) in men or 50 mg/dL (1.3 mmol/L) in women, or on treatment
  • Blood pressure 130/85 or more, or on treatment
  • Fasting glucose 100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L) or more, or on treatment

Reading your result

Zero criteria is the goal. One or two means a marker needs attention before it becomes three. Three or more meets the definition; it does not mean you are ill today, but it is a strong signal to act and to review the numbers with a clinician. The same changes improve every marker at once: more movement, less refined carbohydrate and alcohol, better sleep and, if needed, weight loss of 5–10%.

Limits to keep in mind

Leave out what you do not know; the tool counts only what you enter and tells you how many markers are missing. Lab values should be from a fasting sample for glucose. This is a screening aid, not a diagnosis.

Questions people ask

How common is metabolic syndrome?

Roughly one in three U.S. adults meets the definition, and the share rises with age.

Can metabolic syndrome be reversed?

Yes. Lifestyle change can bring markers back under the cut-offs; many people move from three criteria to fewer within months.

Why does waist count when weight does not?

Waist size reflects fat around the organs, which drives insulin resistance and the other four markers.
