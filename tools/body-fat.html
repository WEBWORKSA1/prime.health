How it works

The U.S. Navy method, developed by Hodgdon and Beckett in 1984, predicts body fat from circumferences. The metric versions are:

Men: %BF = 495 ÷ (1.0324 − 0.19077·log₁₀(waist − neck) + 0.15456·log₁₀(height)) − 450

Women: %BF = 495 ÷ (1.29579 − 0.35004·log₁₀(waist + hip − neck) + 0.22100·log₁₀(height)) − 450

All measurements are in centimetres. How to measure: neck just below the larynx, tape sloping slightly down to the front; waist at the navel for men and at the narrowest point for women, relaxed, after a normal breath out; hips at the widest point of the buttocks. Measure twice and use the average.

Women carry more essential fat for hormonal and reproductive health, which is why the ranges differ. If you add your weight, the calculator splits it into fat mass and lean mass, which is the most useful number to track while training.

Limits to keep in mind

Tape methods typically land within 3–4 percentage points of DEXA or underwater weighing, and errors are larger at very high or very low body fat. Measurement technique causes most of the error, so be consistent: same time of day, same tape, same spots. Watch the trend over weeks rather than a single reading.

Questions people ask

Is the Navy method accurate?

For most people it is within a few percentage points of lab methods, which is comparable to many home scales. It is good for tracking change over time.

Why does it need my neck?

Neck size is a proxy for lean mass in the upper body. Subtracting it from waist (and hip) isolates fat-heavy circumference.

What body fat should I aim for?

For health, the fitness and lower average ranges are a sensible target: about 14–24% for men and 21–31% for women.
