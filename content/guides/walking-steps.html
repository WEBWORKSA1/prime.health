Your phone counts them, your watch buzzes about them, and the 10,000 target is everywhere. But that number started as a slogan for a 1960s Japanese pedometer, not as a scientific finding. Here is what large studies say.

The latest evidence

In 2025, researchers led by the University of Sydney pooled 57 studies of device-measured steps in more than ten countries. Compared with 2,000 steps a day, about 7,000 steps was associated with:

  • 47% lower risk of death from any cause
  • 25% lower risk of developing cardiovascular disease
  • 37% lower risk of dying from cancer
  • 38% lower risk of dementia
  • 22% lower risk of depressive symptoms
  • 28% lower risk of falls

For several outcomes the curve flattened beyond about 7,000; for others, such as type 2 diabetes, benefits continued past 10,000. An earlier 2022 meta-analysis of 15 cohorts found the mortality benefit levelled off around 6,000–8,000 steps for adults over 60 and 8,000–10,000 for younger adults.

What these studies can and cannot tell us

These are observational studies: people who walk more may differ in other ways. Researchers adjust for many factors, and the findings line up with trials showing that walking improves blood pressure, blood sugar and fitness. The safest reading is that more steps help, with the biggest gains at the low end.

Pace matters too

A cadence of about 100 steps per minute corresponds roughly to moderate intensity for most adults; around 130 steps a minute is vigorous. Ten minutes at 100+ steps a minute is about 1,000 brisk steps that also count toward the 150 minutes of moderate activity a week recommended by the World Health Organization.

How to add 3,000 steps without trying hard

  • Walk for 10 minutes after each main meal (about 3,000 steps, and it lowers glucose spikes).
  • Take walking calls and meetings.
  • Get off transit one stop early or park at the far end.
  • Use stairs for anything under four floors.
  • Pace while the kettle boils or during ad breaks.
