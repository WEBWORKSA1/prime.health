Most of what decides how long and how well you live is visible in a handful of numbers. Doctors call them risk factors; we call them your Prime Numbers. Each one has a clear target, each can be measured cheaply, and each responds to what you do this month.

Why numbers beat vague goals

“Eat better” and “get fit” are hard to act on. “Bring my blood pressure from 134/86 to under 130/80” is not. Numbers tell you where you stand, whether a change is working and when to ask for help. They also cut through marketing: no supplement or gadget matters as much as moving these eight.

1. Blood pressure: below 120/80

High blood pressure is the most common modifiable risk factor for heart attack, stroke, kidney disease and dementia. Under the 2025 American Heart Association and American College of Cardiology guideline, below 120/80 mm Hg is normal, 120–129 systolic is elevated, and 130/80 or above is hypertension. Measure at home, seated, after five minutes of rest; average two readings.

2. A1C: below 5.7%

A1C reflects average blood sugar over about three months. Below 5.7% is normal, 5.7–6.4% is the prediabetes range and 6.5% or higher suggests diabetes. Prediabetes rarely causes symptoms, which is why the number matters.

3. Non-HDL cholesterol: below 130 mg/dL (3.4 mmol/L)

Subtract HDL from total cholesterol. The result counts all the particles that can build plaque in arteries. It does not require fasting and is printed on, or easily calculated from, every standard lipid panel.

4. Waist-to-height ratio: below 0.5

Keep your waist to less than half your height. Belly fat around the organs drives insulin resistance and raised blood pressure even in people whose weight looks normal. A tape measure and 30 seconds give you this number.

5. BMI Prime: 0.74 to 1.00

BMI Prime is your BMI divided by 25, the top of the healthy range. It reads like a percentage of the limit: 1.10 means 10% over. BMI is a blunt tool, so read it alongside your waist.

6. VO₂ max: higher every year

Cardiorespiratory fitness is one of the strongest predictors of lifespan. In a meta-analysis of 33 studies, each 1-MET (3.5 mL/kg/min) gain in fitness was associated with about 13% lower risk of death from any cause. You can estimate VO₂ max from your resting pulse or a 12-minute run, and raise it with a mix of easy and hard aerobic training.

7. Sleep: 7 to 9 hours

Sleep researchers recommend at least seven hours a night for adults. Short sleep raises appetite, blood pressure and blood sugar, and blunts mood and focus. Track a week and calculate your sleep debt.

8. Daily steps: about 7,000 or more

A 2025 Lancet Public Health meta-analysis found that about 7,000 steps a day, compared with 2,000, was linked to 47% lower risk of death from any cause, with benefits for heart disease, dementia and depression. Every extra 1,000 steps from a low base counts.
