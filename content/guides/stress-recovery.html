Your body's stress response is a feature, not a bug. A deadline, a hard workout or a difficult conversation raises heart rate and stress hormones to help you rise to the moment. Problems start when the alarm never switches off and recovery never comes.

Stress and recovery, briefly

Acute stress activates the sympathetic nervous system and the hormone cortisol. Recovery is driven by the parasympathetic system: slower heart rate, better digestion, deeper sleep. Chronic stress keeps the first system dominant, which over time can raise blood pressure and blood sugar, disturb sleep and wear down mood.

Exercise is a recovery tool

A 2024 BMJ network meta-analysis of 218 randomised trials found that exercise reduced depressive symptoms, with walking or jogging, yoga, strength training and mixed aerobic exercise among the most effective, and benefits growing with intensity. Exercise also improves sleep and resilience to everyday stress. Even a 10-minute walk outside can shift mood.

Connection is a health number

A 2010 meta-analysis of 148 studies covering more than 300,000 people found that those with stronger social relationships had about 50% higher odds of survival over the study periods, an effect comparable to well-known risk factors such as smoking. Regular contact with friends, family or community groups is genuine health behaviour.

Daily recovery practices

  • Slow breathing: 5 minutes breathing in for 4 seconds and out for 6 shifts the body toward the recovery state.
  • Time outdoors: daylight, green space and walking together.
  • Sleep: protect a consistent 7–9 hours.
  • Boundaries on screens: an evening cut-off for work email and news.
  • Write it down: noting worries and three good things from the day helps many people switch off.

Balance training stress

Hard training is a stressor too. Plan easy days after hard ones, take a lighter week every month or two, and pull back when sleep, mood or resting heart rate are off for several days in a row.

When to get help

See a clinician if low mood, anxiety, irritability or sleep problems last more than two weeks or interfere with work and relationships. Talking therapies and, when appropriate, medicines work well. If you are in crisis or thinking about harming yourself, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line; in the U.S. and Canada you can call or text 988.
