Sleep is when your brain clears waste, your memories consolidate and your hormones reset. It is also the habit people trade away first. Here is the evidence on how much you need and what genuinely helps.

How much sleep adults need

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society reviewed the evidence and concluded that adults should sleep seven or more hours a night on a regular basis. CDC surveys find roughly one in three U.S. adults usually gets less. Needs vary: a few people do well on slightly less, many do best on eight.

What short sleep does to your numbers

  • Appetite: sleep loss shifts hunger hormones and raises cravings for energy-dense food.
  • Blood sugar: even a few short nights reduce insulin sensitivity.
  • Blood pressure: short sleep is linked with higher blood pressure and heart disease risk.
  • Mood and focus: reaction time, memory and emotional control all suffer.

Anchor your body clock

Your internal clock is set mainly by light and timing. The most effective single habit is a fixed wake-up time, seven days a week. Get outdoor daylight within an hour of waking, even on cloudy days, and dim the lights in the last hour before bed.

Habits with good evidence

  • Cut caffeine early: in a sleep-lab study, 400 mg of caffeine taken even six hours before bed reduced total sleep by more than an hour.
  • Go easy on alcohol: it fragments sleep in the second half of the night.
  • Keep the bedroom cool, dark and quiet, and the phone outside it.
  • Exercise regularly, ideally not in the last hour or two before bed if it winds you up.
  • Only go to bed when sleepy; if you are awake and frustrated after about 20 minutes, get up and do something calm in dim light.

Chronic insomnia

Trouble falling or staying asleep at least three nights a week for three months is chronic insomnia. The American College of Physicians recommends cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) as the first treatment. It works through sleep scheduling, stimulus control and changing unhelpful thoughts about sleep, and its benefits last longer than sleeping pills. CBT-I is available through therapists and validated digital programs.

Signs of sleep apnoea

Loud snoring, gasping or pauses in breathing, morning headaches and heavy daytime sleepiness can signal obstructive sleep apnoea, which raises blood pressure and accident risk. It is common and treatable; ask a clinician about a sleep study.

A one-week reset

  1. Pick a wake-up time you can keep every day.
  2. Count back 7.5–8 hours with the sleep cycle calculator to set a target bedtime.
  3. Stop caffeine 8 hours before bed.
  4. Get 10 minutes of daylight each morning.
  5. Log your sleep and check your sleep debt at the end of the week.
