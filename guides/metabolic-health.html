Metabolic health: the five markers to know

Waist, triglycerides, HDL, blood pressure and fasting glucose define metabolic health. Most adults miss at least one. Here is what they mean and what moves them.

Updated November 11, 20268 min read4 sources

Key takeaways

  • Metabolic syndrome means three or more of five markers out of range.
  • A 2019 analysis found only about 12% of U.S. adults were optimal on all five.
  • In the Diabetes Prevention Program, lifestyle change cut progression to diabetes by 58%.
  • Walking after meals, fibre, strength training, sleep and less alcohol all help.

“Metabolic health” sounds abstract, but it comes down to five numbers that any clinic can measure. Together they show how well your body handles fuel, and they predict type 2 diabetes, heart disease and fatty liver years before symptoms appear.

The five markers

MarkerOut of range when
Waist102 cm (40 in) or more in men, 88 cm (35 in) in women; lower cut-offs for Asian populations
Triglycerides150 mg/dL (1.7 mmol/L) or more
HDL cholesterolbelow 40 mg/dL (1.0 mmol/L) in men, 50 mg/dL (1.3 mmol/L) in women
Blood pressure130/85 or more, or on treatment
Fasting glucose100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L) or more, or on treatment

Three or more out of range defines metabolic syndrome under the 2009 harmonized definition.

How common it is

About one in three U.S. adults meets the definition of metabolic syndrome. A 2019 analysis of national survey data found that only about 12% of American adults were optimal on all five markers without medication. Being slim does not guarantee it; neither does being heavy rule it out.

The common root: insulin resistance

When fat accumulates in and around the organs and muscles are under-used, cells respond less to insulin. The pancreas compensates by making more, and over time glucose, triglycerides and blood pressure creep up while HDL falls. That is why the same habits improve all five markers together.

What moves the markers

  • Move after meals. A 10–15 minute walk after eating blunts glucose spikes.
  • Build muscle. Muscle is the largest site of glucose disposal; two strength sessions a week help.
  • Eat more fibre and fewer refined carbohydrates. Beans, oats, vegetables, berries and whole grains slow glucose absorption and lower triglycerides.
  • Cut sugary drinks and limit alcohol. Both raise triglycerides and add visceral fat.
  • Lose 5–10% of body weight if you carry excess. Waist, triglycerides, glucose and blood pressure all improve.
  • Sleep at least seven hours. Short sleep worsens insulin resistance within days.

The strongest evidence

In the U.S. Diabetes Prevention Program, adults with prediabetes who followed a lifestyle program aiming for 7% weight loss and 150 minutes of activity a week cut their risk of developing type 2 diabetes by 58% over about three years, more than the medicine metformin did. Long-term follow-up showed lasting benefit.

Get your numbers

Measure your waist today. Ask for a lipid panel, fasting glucose and A1C at your next check-up, and measure blood pressure at home for a week. Then run the metabolic health check to count your markers and repeat it in three months.

Questions people ask

Can you be thin and metabolically unhealthy?

Yes. Visceral fat, inactivity, genetics and poor sleep can push markers out of range at a normal weight. That is why the markers matter more than the scale.

Do I need a continuous glucose monitor?

Not for most people without diabetes. A fasting glucose and A1C at check-ups, plus your waist, cover the essentials.

How quickly can metabolic markers improve?

Triglycerides and blood sugar can shift within weeks; waist and blood pressure over one to three months.
