VO₂ max and Zone 2: the fitness numbers linked to longer life

Why cardiorespiratory fitness predicts lifespan, what Zone 2 training actually is, and a weekly plan that raises VO₂ max at any age.

Updated November 11, 20268 min read5 sources

Key takeaways

  • Each 1-MET gain in fitness is linked to about 13% lower risk of death from any cause.
  • Zone 2 is easy, conversational training that builds your aerobic engine.
  • One weekly session of hard intervals lifts VO₂ max fastest.
  • A simple week: 3–4 easy sessions, 1 interval session, 2 strength sessions.

If one fitness number deserves a place on your dashboard, it is VO₂ max. It sums up how well your heart, lungs, blood and muscles work together, and it predicts how long people live more strongly than many classic risk factors.

What VO₂ max measures

VO₂ max is the maximum amount of oxygen your body can take in and use, in millilitres per kilogram of body weight per minute. Scientists often express it in METs: 1 MET is resting oxygen use, about 3.5 mL/kg/min. A person with a VO₂ max of 35 has a peak capacity of 10 METs.

It falls with age in everyone, and faster in later decades, but training shifts your whole curve upward. A fit 60-year-old can outscore an inactive 35-year-old.

Why it predicts lifespan

A 2009 JAMA meta-analysis of 33 studies found that each 1-MET higher fitness was associated with about 13% lower risk of death from any cause and 15% lower risk of heart disease events. A 2018 Cleveland Clinic study of more than 120,000 treadmill tests found that people in the lowest fitness group had higher risk of death than people with diabetes, smoking or kidney disease, and that the highest-fitness group fared best, with no upper limit to the benefit. These are observational links, but the pattern is consistent and the biology is plausible.

Zone 2, explained

Zone 2 is steady, easy aerobic exercise: roughly 60–70% of maximum heart rate, where you can talk in full sentences but would rather not sing. At this intensity your muscles rely heavily on fat and on the mitochondria that burn it. Spending time here grows mitochondria and capillaries and improves how efficiently you clear lactate. It is also gentle enough to repeat most days without piling up fatigue.

Use the talk test or a heart-rate monitor. Brisk walking uphill, cycling, rowing, swimming or easy jogging all work.

Intervals: the fastest VO₂ max lever

Hard efforts push the heart to pump its maximum volume. A classic protocol from Norwegian research is 4 × 4 minutes at a hard effort (about 85–95% of maximum heart rate) with 3 minutes of easy recovery between. In a 2007 trial, this format improved VO₂ max more than the same total work done at moderate intensity. One session a week is enough for most people; two for those chasing performance.

A simple weekly plan

DaySession
MondayStrength training, 40 minutes
TuesdayZone 2, 40–60 minutes
WednesdayWalk or rest
ThursdayIntervals: warm-up, 4 × 4 minutes hard, cool-down
FridayStrength training, 40 minutes
SaturdayLong Zone 2, 60–90 minutes
SundayEasy walk

That totals roughly 150–200 minutes of moderate and 30–40 minutes of vigorous activity, well within World Health Organization recommendations. Beginners can start with half the volume and build by about 10% a week.

Tracking progress

Estimate VO₂ max every three months with the same method, ideally the 12-minute test on a flat track or your resting heart rate measured on waking. Watch for a falling resting heart rate and a faster pace at the same heart rate in Zone 2; both are signs your aerobic engine is growing.

Safety first

If you have heart disease, chest pain on exertion, fainting episodes or take medicines that affect heart rate, get medical advice before hard intervals. Stop and seek care for chest pain, unusual breathlessness or palpitations.

Questions people ask

Is Zone 2 better than high-intensity training?

They do different jobs. Zone 2 builds endurance and is easy to recover from; intervals raise VO₂ max quickly. The best results come from combining both.

Can older adults improve VO₂ max?

Yes. Trials in people in their 60s and 70s show meaningful gains from supervised aerobic and interval training. Start gradually and get medical advice if you have heart disease.

How long until VO₂ max improves?

Measurable gains often appear within 6–12 weeks of consistent training.
