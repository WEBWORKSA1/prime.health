How to read health news without getting fooled

A practical checklist for judging health headlines: study type, absolute versus relative risk, who was studied and who paid. Use it on every story in Health Pulse.

Updated November 11, 20266 min read3 sources

Key takeaways

  • Ask what kind of study it was: lab, animal, observational or randomised trial.
  • Look for absolute risk, not just relative risk.
  • Check who was studied and how many people.
  • One study rarely changes advice; guidelines weigh all of them.

Every week brings a headline that coffee, eggs or red wine will save or shorten your life. Here is a quick way to judge any health story, including the ones in our Health Pulse feed.

1. What kind of study was it?

Study typeWhat it can show
Cell or animal studyA possible mechanism. Often does not translate to people.
Observational studyAn association. Cannot prove cause on its own.
Randomised controlled trialCause and effect for the people and time frame studied.
Systematic review and meta-analysisThe combined picture across studies; strongest when based on good trials.

2. Relative or absolute risk?

“Doubles your risk” sounds alarming. If the risk goes from 1 in 10,000 to 2 in 10,000, the absolute increase is 1 in 10,000. Good reporting gives both. When only relative risk is given, ask: out of how many?

3. Who was studied, and how many?

Results in elite athletes, older men or people with a disease may not apply to you. Small studies, especially those with fewer than a hundred people, are prone to chance findings.

4. What was measured?

Did the study track real outcomes such as heart attacks and deaths, or surrogate markers like a blood test? Surrogates are useful but do not always predict outcomes.

5. Who paid, and who is selling?

Industry funding does not make a study wrong, but it is worth knowing. Be wary when the story ends with a product link.

6. Does it fit the bigger picture?

One surprising result rarely overturns a large body of evidence. Guidelines change slowly for a reason: they weigh all the studies. If a headline contradicts long-standing advice, wait for confirmation.

The 30-second checklist

  1. Human or animal?
  2. Trial or observation?
  3. Absolute risk given?
  4. How many people, and like me?
  5. Real outcome or marker?
  6. Who funded it?
  7. Does it agree with other studies?

Questions people ask

Why do studies seem to contradict each other?

Different designs, populations and measures give different answers, and random chance plays a part. Systematic reviews pool studies to find the signal.

Are preprints reliable?

Preprints have not been peer reviewed. They can be valuable but deserve extra caution.

How does Prime.Health choose sources?

Our guides lean on guidelines and systematic reviews first. Health Pulse lists headlines from public research and health agencies; inclusion is not endorsement.
